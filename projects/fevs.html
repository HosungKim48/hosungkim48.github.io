Does getting the training you need keep people from leaving?

Organizations spend heavily on training but rarely know whether it keeps people. Using 15 years of the U.S. Federal Employee Viewpoint Survey, I asked whether employees who feel well trained are less likely to plan to leave, and whether agencies that improve on development see less leaving afterward.

01

The question and the data

Training is usually judged by how people feel right after it, and research shows those reactions say little about learning. Data that links training to outcomes is almost always internal to companies. FEVS is the largest public alternative: several hundred thousand federal employees a year, asked about training, development, their supervisors and whether they plan to leave.

Training (employees)

Agreement with “I receive the training I need to do my job well.” This is perceived training adequacy: how trained people feel, not tested skill. In 2024, 67% answered positively (n = 666,259).

Development (agencies)

The training item starts in 2022, too late for a 15-year panel. Agencies are tracked on “I am given a real opportunity to improve my skills,” asked every year. The two move together across agencies (r = 0.71–0.85).

Outcome

“Are you considering leaving your organization within the next year?” Any “yes” counts. This is intent, not actual departures, and every result is an association, not proof of cause.

02

Feeling well trained goes with staying, but less than it first looks

The raw pattern is steep. But people who feel good about their job tend to rate everything higher, so I compared employees in the same agency who also rated their supervisor’s support, recognition, workload and pay alike. The gap shrinks from about 23 points to about 5, and I headline the cautious number.

03

Agencies that improved on development didn’t see less leaving

Agencies with better development climates do have lower leaving (r = −0.45 across 15-year averages). But that could just mean some agencies are better places to work for years at a time. So I compared each agency with itself and asked whether a rise in its score came before a fall in leaving.

A rise in an agency’s development score was not followed by less leaving (−0.02 per point, 95% CI −0.15 to 0.11, bootstrap p = 0.81), a precise null: at most about 0.3 points either way. It was followed by slightly higher satisfaction (bootstrap p = 0.004), a smaller, same-survey finding.

29 agencies, agency and year fixed effects; bars are 95% intervals; wild cluster bootstrap (9,999 draws)

Reverse direction

High leaving this year predicts a lower development score next year just as strongly (−0.58 vs −0.48), so the year-ahead pattern can’t show which comes first.

Horse race

Once next year’s score is known, this year’s score adds nothing detectable to predicting next year’s leaving (−0.08, CI −0.22 to 0.07).

Changes on changes

An agency’s rise in score doesn’t predict a fall in leaving the next year, with or without each agency’s own long-run trend, and without small agencies.

04

What this means for HR leaders

Employees’ sense of being well trained is a useful warning signal for retention risk, especially among newer staff. On this evidence, raising that score is not a lever that lowers turnover intent on its own.

01

A signal, not a lever

Use perceived training adequacy to flag where retention risk is building, not as a promise that a new program will cut attrition.

02

Target the first 20 years

The link is strongest for newer and mid-career staff, and for people weighing another job in the same sector: internal mobility is at stake.

03

Don’t promise turnover cuts from a survey score

Agencies whose development scores rose saw slightly higher satisfaction, not lower intent to leave.

04

Measure what training builds

Test skill before, right after and 3–6 months after training, roll programs out in stages so later groups act as a comparison, and link training records to real separations, moves and promotions.

My role

  • Chose the question, wrote the research proposal and made the calls that shape what the report claims: which survey questions to use, how to treat the 2020 COVID survey year, headlining the conservative estimate, and leading with the null result.
  • Directed Claude Code (an AI coding agent), which wrote and ran the data pipeline, the models and the first draft of the report; I reviewed and approved each of four milestones, with every decision and its reason logged.
  • The project is fully reproducible: six numbered scripts rebuild everything from OPM’s public files, the report is filled from a template so no number is typed by hand, and the pipeline reproduces OPM’s published 2024 figures for 4 agencies to within 0.5 points.

Methods: Survey-weighted logistic regression · average marginal effects · fixed-effects panels · first differences · wild cluster bootstrap · robustness and reverse-causality checks
